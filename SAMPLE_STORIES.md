# Sample Playthrough Stories

Five vastly different journeys through the same broken world. Each represents a possible game experience - different starting conditions, convoy compositions, goals, and fates.

These stories will help us:
1. Discover what mechanics emerge naturally
2. Understand what makes a journey compelling
3. Identify encounter types and decision points
4. Build our NPC roster from characters who appear
5. Find the emotional core of the game

---

## Story One: "The Pediatric Ward"

### Setup
Dr. Amara Okonkwo was working the night shift in the pediatric oncology ward when the hospital generators failed for the final time. The backup batteries gave them six hours. In that time, she made a choice: load every child she could save into whatever vehicles she could find in the parking garage, and drive toward the rumored medical convoy heading for the CDC remnants in Atlanta.

### The Convoy
- **Lead Vehicle**: Stolen hospital shuttle van (medical equipment, three beds)
- **Second**: Minivan belonging to a parent who refused to leave (driven by father Marcus, carrying his daughter Lily and two other children)
- **Third**: Dr. Okonkwo's personal Subaru (medical supplies, another nurse named Dev)
- **Acquired Later**: School bus found abandoned with keys in ignition

### The Journey
Starting from a hospital outside Memphis. Five children ranging from 8-14, three with ongoing treatment needs. Limited medication supply - maybe two weeks if rationed. The CDC facility is a rumor passed through ham radio, unconfirmed.

**Week One**: Interstate is a graveyard of vehicles. They navigate by pushing cars aside, burning precious fuel. Marcus's daughter Lily has a seizure - they use medication they can't replace. At a truck stop, they encounter a family hiding in the restaurant. The father, a former EMT named Wendell, joins when he sees the children. His wife Patrice won't go without their dogs - two German Shepherds. Do they have room?

*Decision: Take the dogs (morale boost, possible protection, consume food) or leave the family behind (lose EMT skills, lose potential allies)?*

They take the dogs. The shepherds prove their worth two days later when raiders approach at night - the barking gives them time to flee.

**Week Two**: A child named Omar needs dialysis. There is no dialysis. Dev the nurse knows a hospital in Tupelo might still have supplies - it's thirty miles off route, through territory marked with warning signs spray-painted by other survivors: "SICKNESS."

*Decision: Risk the detour for Omar, or keep moving and manage his decline?*

They detour. Tupelo is quiet in the wrong way. Houses with X's painted on doors. They find the hospital picked clean except for one wing that was chained shut from outside. Inside: supplies, but also understanding of why this place was abandoned. They take what they need and burn their clothes afterward. Omar gets another month.

**Week Three**: Outside Birmingham, they find the "medical convoy" - it's real but not what they hoped. Eighteen vehicles of desperate people following a veterinarian and a retired army medic. No oncologists. No pediatric specialists. But there's safety in numbers and a destination: a naval base on the Gulf Coast where ships are supposedly evacuating people to offshore platforms.

Dr. Okonkwo has to choose: Join the convoy and share her supplies across sixty people, or stay small and fast and keep her children's odds higher?

*Decision: Merge convoys or stay independent?*

She compromises. They travel alongside but keep separate supplies. An uneasy alliance. The vet, a gruff woman named Carolina, thinks Amara is hoarding. Amara thinks Carolina is rationing hope she doesn't have.

**Week Four**: One of the children, a 12-year-old named Bao, has been improving. Against all odds. He starts helping Dev with supply inventory, shows an aptitude. He asks Dr. Okonkwo if he can learn to help, really help. She starts teaching him. It might be pointless. It might be the most important thing.

Two days from the coast, raiders hit the combined convoy. Real ones this time - organized, armed, demanding "tribute." The vet wants to negotiate. A hotheaded survivor named Jackson wants to fight. Marcus, quiet Marcus who has barely spoken, reveals he did two tours in Afghanistan. He doesn't want to fight. He will if he has to.

*Decision: Negotiate (lose supplies), fight (risk lives), or attempt to flee in the night (abandon the larger convoy)?*

They fight. They win, barely. Jackson is wounded badly. Carolina the vet operates on a human for the first time. Marcus won't look at his hands for days afterward. One of the raiders, shot in the stomach and dying, is maybe fifteen years old. Dr. Okonkwo tries to save him. She can't.

**Ending**: They reach the coast. There are no ships. The naval base is empty, abandoned months ago. But there's infrastructure. Fences. Generators that could work. Clean water access. The convoy stops running. They start building.

Lily dies in month three. The medication ran out. Marcus stays anyway. He helps build the clinic. Bao becomes its first trained assistant, then later, its first new doctor.

The settlement grows. Other convoys arrive. Some are turned away. Some are welcomed. Dr. Okonkwo has to decide, over and over, who they can save and who they can't.

### Themes Emerged
- Medical resource scarcity
- Children as both burden and purpose
- Expertise becomes currency
- The weight of triage decisions
- Building something vs. just surviving
- Unlikely community formation

## Story Two: "The Repo Men"

### Setup
Luis Machado repossessed cars for eleven years. He knew how to find them, take them, and disappear before anyone knew what happened. When the banks stopped existing and money became memory, his skills translated. Now he acquires vehicles for a mobile settlement of mechanics, welders, and scrappers who call themselves The Shop. They don't raid. They trade. They fix. And sometimes they take cars from people who won't be needing them anymore.

### The Convoy
- **Lead**: Modified tow truck with winch and crane ("La Garra")
- **Support**: Box truck converted to mobile workshop
- **Scout**: Two motorcycles running rotation
- **Transport**: Whatever they're moving that week
- **The Shop's Main Body**: Somewhere behind them, a caravan of eighteen vehicles carrying forty-three people

### The Journey
Not a single direction but a circuit. The Shop moves in a long loop through what used to be Texas, Oklahoma, and New Mexico, following rumors of intact vehicles and settlements that need mechanical help. Luis runs the acquisition crew - four people who go ahead, make deals, and sometimes make recoveries.

**Month One**: They're in the hill country when the radio crackles - a settlement near Fredericksburg has a working pre-war ambulance but no one who can fix it. Classic trade setup. Luis takes La Garra and the box truck. With him: Chen, who can fix anything electrical; Yolanda, who handles negotiation and carries a shotgun she's never fired; and a new kid named Dustin who won't say where he came from but knows engines like he was raised inside one.

The settlement is nervous. They've been hit twice by a group calling themselves The Claim - raiders who take "property tax" from anyone in their territory. The ambulance is leverage; if they can get it running, they can outrun trouble.

*Decision: Fix the ambulance for standard trade, or negotiate for more given the danger in the area?*

Yolanda negotiates hard. They need food, and this settlement has a working greenhouse. The deal: fix the ambulance, receive two months of vegetables, and The Shop will add this settlement to their circuit - regular maintenance visits in exchange for ongoing food supply. A relationship, not a transaction.

Dustin fixes the ambulance in six hours. Chen says he's never seen anyone work like that. When they're leaving, Dustin asks if they can wait - there's a car he knows about, a few miles out, belonged to someone who "doesn't need it." Luis doesn't ask questions. The car is a vintage El Camino, engine pristine. Dustin sits in the driver's seat for a long time before starting it. They don't talk about it.

**Month Two**: The Claim finds them. Not raiders in the movie sense - organized, patient, running their territory like a business. Their leader, a woman named Constance who used to be a county tax assessor, explains calmly that The Shop is operating in Claim territory and owes back taxes. She's not threatening. She's bureaucratic. Somehow that's worse.

*Decision: Pay the "tax" (lose significant supplies), refuse and face consequences, or negotiate a different arrangement?*

Luis does something unexpected. He offers a deal. The Claim has vehicles - lots of them, seized from "tax delinquents." Most are barely running. The Shop could fix them. A percentage of restored vehicles stays with The Claim. The rest become Shop property. Partnership.

Constance considers for three days. During those three days, The Shop is "guests" - comfortable but watched. Chen finds a radio in the house they're given, hidden, broadcasting their conversations. Yolanda befriends a Claim member named Douglas who drinks too much and talks about how he ended up here - they took his family's farm, then offered him a job. He doesn't know if he's a prisoner or an employee.

Constance agrees to a trial arrangement. The Shop will restore ten vehicles. They keep four. The Claim keeps six. If it works, they'll talk about more.

**Month Three**: The work goes well until Dustin finds something. One of the vehicles they're restoring has a child's car seat in the back, still stained. The registration belongs to a family name that matches missing person notices posted in three settlements. Dustin brings it to Luis. Chen wants to ignore it - they're guests, they have a deal, they can't afford trouble. Yolanda wants to find out what happened to the family.

*Decision: Investigate and risk the partnership, or let it go?*

Luis investigates quietly. The family - mother, father, two kids - passed through Claim territory four months ago. They couldn't pay the tax. The official story is they were escorted to the territory border and released. Douglas, drunk again, tells a different story. Constance's number two, a man named Wheeler, has a side operation. Certain "delinquents" are sold to labor camps further west. The family is probably in an oil field in the Permian Basin, working off a debt that compounds faster than they can pay.

**Month Four**: Luis makes a choice that splits The Shop. He takes La Garra, Chen, Yolanda, and Dustin. They leave at night. They're going to the Permian Basin. The rest of The Shop continues the circuit without them.

The labor camp is exactly as bad as advertised. Fences, guards, people working oil equipment that shouldn't still be running. The family is there. So are forty others. The camp is run by a man named Briggs who sells fuel to anyone with enough to trade. In a world short on gasoline, he's built an empire on the back of captive labor.

*Decision: Attempt a rescue (massive risk), try to buy the family's freedom (do they have enough?), or document and leave (save themselves, maybe alert others)?*

They don't have enough to buy anyone. They can't fight an armed camp. Luis comes up with a repo man solution. They take Briggs's vehicles. All of them. Every truck, tanker, and transport. In one night, using every skill they have, they disable, drain, or steal every vehicle in the compound. The guards wake up to an oil operation with no way to move product.

In the chaos, people walk away. The family. Others. Not everyone - some are too broken, too scared, or the guards rally too fast. But enough.

**Ending**: Luis can never go back to Claim territory. The partnership is dead. Briggs puts a bounty on La Garra that follows them for years. But The Shop gains something else - a reputation. The crew that doesn't just fix cars. The crew that takes them from people who shouldn't have them.

The family joins The Shop. The mother, Teresa, becomes their best negotiator. One of the kids grows up to lead the motorcycle scouts. Dustin finally talks about the El Camino - it was his parents' car. They didn't make it. He did.

### Themes Emerged
- Skills as survival currency
- Moral complexity of post-collapse economics
- When does trade become exploitation?
- Chosen family vs. found community
- The persistence of systems and bureaucracy
- Reputation as resource

## Story Three: "Northbound"

### Setup
The heat came first. Then the crops died. Then the aquifers went dry. South Texas became unlivable in a single summer. Maria Santos was a high school principal in Laredo. When the last water truck didn't come, she took the school's two buses, filled them with whoever would come, and pointed north. The rumor was Canada - still water, still cold, still alive. It's two thousand miles away. They have enough gas for maybe four hundred.

### The Convoy
- **Bus One**: Yellow school bus, carries twenty-six people, most of them families with children
- **Bus Two**: Same model, carries eighteen people plus supplies, driven by the school janitor Hector who kept both buses running for fifteen years
- **Scout Car**: Maria's personal Honda Civic, usually driven ahead by her nephew Alejandro

### The Journey
Starting from the edge of habitability and driving toward hope. The passengers are mostly Maria's community - former students now adults, their families, people who trusted her with their children for years. Also among them: strangers who made it to the school before they left, including a man named Garrett who claims to be a geologist and talks about the aquifer collapse like it was personal.

**Week One**: Interstate 35 is a corridor of the dead. Thousands had the same idea months earlier. The buses weave between vehicles that didn't make it - overheated, out of gas, or simply stopped when there was nowhere left to go. The children are told not to look out the windows in certain stretches.

Fuel is the first crisis. Alejandro scouts a truck stop outside San Antonio - picked clean except for tanks underground that no one could access without power. Garrett says he can get it. He's done this before, he says. He's vague about where and when.

*Decision: Trust the stranger with their only chance at fuel, or spend another day searching for alternatives?*

Garrett delivers. Hand pump, patience, and knowledge of where the fuel actually sits in those underground tanks. They get ninety gallons of diesel - weeks of range. Maria asks how he knew. Garrett says he used to work in oil and gas infrastructure. He helped build the systems that are now everyone's tombs. He doesn't seem proud.

**Week Two**: They pick up a woman walking alone outside Austin. She's dehydrated to the point of hallucination, carrying a backpack with photographs and a laptop with a dead battery. Her name is Dr. Simone Adler, and she's a climate scientist. She was at a university research station when the funding dried up and the staff scattered. She kept working alone, documenting, until she couldn't anymore.

Simone has information - models, data, predictions. She knows where water might still exist. She knows where it definitely doesn't. But she also knows something else: this heat pattern, this drought, it's not stopping. The line of habitability is moving north at roughly fifty miles per year. Even Canada is temporary.

*Decision: Share this knowledge with the convoy (causing potential despair) or keep it private (protect morale with ignorance)?*

Maria chooses to know. She sits with Simone and Garrett - the scientist and the infrastructure man - and they talk through the night about what's real. In the morning, she tells the convoy this: "We're not running to safety. There is no safety. We're running to time. More years in a livable place. That's what we're looking for. That's worth finding."

Some people take it hard. A family leaves at the next opportunity, heading east toward relatives they hope are still alive. The rest stay. Something shifts - they're not refugees anymore. They're migrants. There's a difference.

**Week Three**: Dallas is a ruin. Not destroyed - just empty. A city of millions, abandoned over months as the infrastructure failed system by system. The convoy drives through downtown in silence. Shopping carts in the middle of streets. Doors hanging open. A billboard advertising water delivery service, phone number tagged with spray paint: "THEY NEVER CAME."

In a north Dallas suburb, they find people. A settlement of maybe two hundred, living in a strip mall, surviving on rainwater collection and what they've scavenged. They're not hostile, but they're not welcoming either. Resources are calculated to the drop. They don't have extra for fifty-plus mouths.

The settlement leader is a former city councilwoman named Patricia Vance. She's kept these people alive through organization and hard rationing. She sees Maria's convoy as a threat, then as an opportunity. She has a proposal: some of her people want to leave, to try for the north. Patricia won't let them take supplies - it would doom the settlement. But if Maria takes them...

*Decision: Take on more people (stretch resources thinner) in exchange for Patricia's intelligence about the route north?*

Maria agrees. Twelve new passengers join, including Patricia's own son David, who his mother both wants to save and can't bear to see leave. The goodbye is brief and brutal. David will carry it for years.

The intelligence is worth it: Patricia has radio contact with settlements along the route. She gives Maria frequencies, names, warnings. A town in Oklahoma that shoots strangers. A bridge in Kansas that's been deliberately collapsed by a group that charges ferry fees. A stretch of Nebraska where convoys have vanished without radio distress - no one knows why.

**Week Four**: The Oklahoma town is as bad as advertised. They approach with white flags. The response is gunfire over their heads. They turn back, find another route, lose two days and a quarter tank of fuel.

In southern Kansas, the weather changes. Rain. Real rain, not the sporadic dusty drizzle of the south. People cry. The children dance in it. Hector lets the buses sit and fill every container they have.

The collapsed bridge is real too. The "ferry" is a group of opportunists with a working barge. The price is steep: fuel, food, or people. They'll take workers for a week in exchange for passage.

*Decision: Pay in supplies they can't spare, give up people temporarily (trusting they'll be released), or find another crossing?*

Maria won't trade people. She won't pay their price. Garrett speaks up - he knows rivers. He spent years working pipeline crossings. If they go upstream, there might be a railroad bridge, something built for weight.

They find it. A freight rail bridge, questionable but standing. The buses are too heavy to cross safely. They unload everything and everyone. People walk the supplies across by hand. Hector drives Bus One across at two miles per hour, alone, ready to jump. It holds. Bus Two follows. They reload on the other side.

The ferry operators watch from downstream. They don't interfere. One of them, a young man, radios ahead to warn whoever will listen: "Two buses, headed north, well organized. Didn't pay."

**Week Five**: Nebraska. The stretch where convoys disappear. They drive during the day only. They stay close. They keep watches at night.

On day three, they find out why convoys vanish. It's not raiders. It's the land. Sinkholes - massive, sudden, hidden by grass. An underground water system collapsed years ago, leaving cavities that open without warning. They find a convoy that fell - three vehicles in a pit forty feet deep, people still inside who starved waiting for rescue that never came.

Alejandro scouts on foot, throwing rocks ahead, mapping safe passage. It takes a week to cross thirty miles.

**Ending**: They reach the Minnesota border in early fall. The first frost comes and people cheer. Cold means water stays. Cold means things grow differently but they still grow.

They don't make it to Canada. They find a town instead - Willmar, Minnesota, population pre-collapse around twenty thousand, now around eight hundred, actively looking for people. Not just accepting - recruiting. They need farmers, teachers, anyone who can work. Maria has fifty people who can work.

The town council offers them housing. An abandoned neighborhood, houses needing repair but standing. Maria negotiates: her people stay together. They keep their buses. They're not absorbed - they're allied.

Simone sets up a weather station, keeps modeling, keeps warning. Garrett helps rebuild water infrastructure, finally using his knowledge to help instead of harm. Maria becomes something she never expected - not a principal, not a convoy leader, but a representative. Her people to the town. The town to other convoys arriving behind them.

The fifty-mile-per-year line keeps moving north. They know they'll move again someday. But not today.

### Themes Emerged
- Climate as the slow apocalypse
- Migration as permanent state
- Knowledge (scientific and practical) as survival tool
- The journey changing the travelers
- Finding "good enough" vs. searching for perfect
- Leadership as burden and responsibility

## Story Four: "The Broadcast"

### Setup
Eleanor Chen was the overnight DJ at a small radio station in Bakersfield when the first quake hit. Not the Big One everyone expected - something stranger, a cascade of failures along fault lines that shouldn't have moved together. The station had an emergency generator. Eleanor stayed on the air because she didn't know what else to do. She became a voice in the dark for thousands, then hundreds, then dozens. When the fuel ran out and the signal died, the people who'd been listening came to find her.

### The Convoy
- **Mobile Station**: Former news van with broadcast equipment, generator running on converted vegetable oil
- **Support Vehicle**: Pickup truck carrying fuel and parts
- **The Listeners**: A changing group of vehicles, usually five to fifteen, following the broadcast

### The Journey
Not a destination - a mission. Eleanor broadcasts as they travel, sharing information between settlements, reuniting families, warning about dangers. The convoy moves in whatever direction the stories take them. They're not refugees. They're infrastructure.

**Month One**: The broadcast range is maybe forty miles on a good day. Eleanor learns what people need: not entertainment, not news in the old sense, but connection. She reads names - people looking for people. She describes road conditions. She tells stories submitted by listeners, memories of the world before.

The first real test comes when a listener named Marcus radios in with coordinates and a message: twenty people trapped in a collapsed parking structure, injured, no way out. It's sixty miles away, outside their range. Do they go?

*Decision: Break from their route to attempt rescue, or broadcast the message and hope someone closer responds?*

They go. The rescue is brutal - two days of digging with hand tools. Seven people are alive when they break through. Thirteen aren't. Marcus, the caller, isn't there - he was never there. He died in the collapse but his last act was rigging a radio to broadcast on repeat, hoping someone would hear.

Eleanor does a special broadcast from the site. She names all twenty. She tells their story. The seven survivors join the convoy. One of them, a structural engineer named Yuki, becomes essential.

**Month Two**: The convoy picks up an unexpected listener: a group calling themselves the New California Republic, which is less grand than it sounds - about sixty people in a defended compound trying to rebuild governmental structure. Their leader, a former state assemblyman named Rodriguez, wants to hire Eleanor. Official broadcaster. Salary in supplies. Protection guaranteed.

*Decision: Join the NCR and gain stability, or stay independent and keep the broadcast neutral?*

Eleanor turns him down. The broadcast works because it's not owned. If she becomes NCR's voice, she becomes propaganda. Rodriguez is offended but not stupid - he lets her go. He also stops sharing information with her, and the NCR becomes a gap in her coverage.

**Month Three**: The real danger isn't raiders or disasters - it's other broadcasters. A signal starts appearing on adjacent frequencies. A man calling himself "Truth" with a different kind of broadcast: coordinates of "weak" settlements, schedules of "vulnerable" convoys, information that helps predators find prey. He's monetizing chaos.

Eleanor's listeners tell her about "Truth." They want her to do something. She's a DJ, not a soldier. What can she do?

*Decision: Ignore Truth and focus on positive broadcasting, actively counter his information with warnings, or try to find and stop him?*

She fights him the only way she knows how. Every time Truth broadcasts a target, Eleanor broadcasts a warning. When he gives coordinates, she gives counter-coordinates - false information to confuse anyone listening. When he describes a settlement's defenses, she describes them differently.

It becomes a war of signals. Truth gets angry - his broadcasts turn personal, threatening. Eleanor keeps her voice calm, professional, the same voice that read late-night requests in the old world. Her listeners start hunting. Not because she asked - because they're her community now, and he's attacking their DJ.

**Month Four**: A listener named Tomás radios in. He's found Truth. The man's real name is Kevin Marsh, former data analyst, broadcasting from a fortified house in the Mojave. He's protected by a group that profits from his information. Tomás has coordinates but not numbers - he doesn't know how many guns are in that house.

*Decision: Share the coordinates publicly (risk a massacre either direction), keep them private and plan carefully, or let it go and hope Truth eventually burns out?*

Eleanor doesn't broadcast the coordinates. Instead, she does something else. She finds out everything she can about Kevin Marsh. His life before. His family. His story. Then she broadcasts that.

Not threats. Not targeting information. Just: who he is. Who he was. The wife who died. The son who was taken by raiders. The break that turned a data analyst into Truth. She makes him human on the airwaves, complex and sad and specific.

Kevin Marsh's next broadcast is erratic, emotional. Then it stops. Two weeks later, Tomás radios in again. The house is empty. Kevin Marsh is gone - not dead, just gone. Someone saw him on the road, alone, heading north.

**Month Five**: The broadcast has become something larger than Eleanor. Other voices are joining - local DJs in settlements that have rebuilt power, sharing her frequencies, creating a network. She's not the only voice anymore. She's the first voice, the one who started it, but there are others now.

A new problem emerges: her generator is dying. The vegetable oil conversion was always a patch, and the engine is giving out. Without the generator, no mobile broadcast. Without the broadcast...

Yuki the engineer says she can fix it, maybe, if they can find specific parts. Those parts might exist in Los Angeles. Los Angeles, which everyone has avoided, which burns periodically, which is carved into a dozen hostile territories.

*Decision: Risk LA for the parts, settle in one place and broadcast from there, or accept that the mobile era is ending and transition the network to others?*

They go to LA. Three vehicles, ten people, into the territory everyone avoids. What they find isn't what they expected - parts of the city are dead, parts are war zones, but parts are... functioning. Communities in high-rises with solar panels. A water district that kept working through everything. A hospital that never closed, just transformed.

LA isn't dead. It's metamorphosed into something unrecognizable but alive.

They find the parts. They also find listeners - thousands who've been picking up her signal when atmospheric conditions allowed, who know her voice, who organized around her broadcasts without her knowing.

**Ending**: Eleanor doesn't leave LA. The convoy settles in Glendale, in a community that offers them a permanent broadcast location - a rooftop studio with solar power and a proper antenna. She becomes the hub of the network she built.

The mobile broadcast becomes something new - other DJs who travel, feeding her information, extending her range. Her voice still carries, but now it has echoes. In the fragmented frequencies of post-collapse California, something like communication infrastructure is rebuilding itself, one broadcast at a time.

On her final mobile broadcast, Eleanor says: "I've been your late-night companion for six months. I came from a small station in Bakersfield where I played requests for insomniacs. I never expected to be here. None of us expected any of this. But we're here, and we're talking, and that means we're still a civilization. As long as we can hear each other, we exist. This is Eleanor Chen, signing off mobile operations. But I'll see you on the dial. I'll be the one telling stories about who we were and who we're becoming."

### Themes Emerged
- Information as resource/weapon
- Community building through communication
- Neutrality vs. engagement
- How societies rebuild (infrastructure of connection)
- Non-violent conflict resolution
- Collective identity formation

## Story Five: "The Inheritance"

### Setup
When Raymond Price died, he left his three adult children something unusual: coordinates, a key, and instructions to "get there together or don't bother." Raymond was a difficult man - paranoid, secretive, wealthy in a way his children never understood. They hadn't spoken to each other in years. Now they're driving into the Nevada desert together, following the map of a man they barely knew, hoping whatever's at the end is worth the trip.

### The Convoy
- **Vehicle One**: Older sister Diane's SUV, practical, packed carefully
- **Vehicle Two**: Middle brother Marcus's vintage muscle car, impractical, fast
- **Vehicle Three**: Youngest brother James's hybrid sedan, nearly useless in a world without charging stations

### The Journey
Starting from a lawyer's office in Reno, three weeks after everything fell apart. The world is ending, and they're chasing an inheritance. Each of them needs it to mean different things.

**Day One**: The reading of the will happened in a bunker. The lawyer, a woman named Stern who clearly knew more than she was saying, gave them the coordinates, the key, and one piece of advice: "Your father wanted you to have this. He also wanted you to earn it. The journey matters."

The siblings haven't been in the same room in four years, since their mother's funeral. Diane is 45, a corporate consultant, controlled and practical. Marcus is 42, a contractor, rough-edged and resentful. James is 38, a software developer, youngest child syndrome incarnate. They blame each other for things that happened decades ago. They blame Raymond for everything.

*Decision: Travel together (father's instructions, safety in numbers, no trust) or split up and race to the coordinates?*

They travel together. Not because they want to - because none of them trust the others to share what's there.

**Day Two**: James's hybrid dies outside Fallon. No charge stations, battery backup exhausted. They argue for an hour about whether to abandon it. James packed poorly - electronics, clothes, no food. He assumed civilization would still work.

Marcus wants to leave him. Diane says they can't - father's instructions said together. James says nothing, staring at his useless car, realizing for the first time that the world has actually changed.

*Decision: Abandon James's car and redistribute (cramped, supplies limited), tow it (slow, uses fuel), or leave James behind?*

They redistribute. Marcus takes supplies and fuel from James's car with aggressive efficiency, making sure James sees every item packed wrong, every decision criticized. Diane mediates. She's been mediating between them their whole lives.

The coordinates point to somewhere past Tonopah, into wilderness none of them know. Raymond loved the desert. His children don't.

**Day Three**: Outside Tonopah, they encounter others for the first time. A group of six travelers heading west, away from whatever is at the coordinates. One of them, an older woman, recognizes the key Diane is carrying. "Price gave that to you? You're his kids?"

The woman knew Raymond. He passed through weeks ago, before he died, checking on something. She doesn't know what's out there, but she knows it's real. "He talked about you three," she says. "Said he'd failed you. Said this might fix it. Personally? I think he was crazy. But he was crazy in a way that built things."

*Decision: Press the woman for more information (she seems willing to trade), or continue following the coordinates blind?*

They trade. Marcus has cigarettes (he quit years ago, kept a pack). The woman shares what she knows: Raymond had a compound, built over decades, stocked for exactly this scenario. He wasn't just paranoid - he was prepared. But there's a catch. The compound is designed to only open for the three of them together. Some kind of biometric system. Raymond's final manipulation from beyond the grave.

**Day Four**: They reach the coordinates. There's nothing there - desert, rocks, a dry wash. James says it's a joke. Marcus blames Diane for the navigation. Diane walks in circles, looking for anything.

Then James notices it: a seam in a rock face, almost invisible, with a small panel beside it. The key fits. But nothing happens.

The panel has three spaces. Three thumbprints. All three, together, or nothing.

They stand there, in the desert, at the door to whatever Raymond built, and they can't bring themselves to all touch it at once. The resentments are too deep. The anger too old.

*Decision: Work through enough conflict to cooperate, or walk away from whatever's inside?*

They talk. For the first time in decades, they actually talk. Marcus says what he's never said: that Raymond favored him as a child and it was a curse, expectations he could never meet, love that felt like evaluation. Diane says what she's never said: that she raised them both, that their mother checked out and she became the parent at twelve years old, that she's tired. James says what he's never said: that being the youngest meant being invisible, that Raymond barely knew he existed, that he became successful in tech to get his father's attention and it never worked.

The desert doesn't care about their trauma. The sun is setting. They can work through this or they can die out here.

**Ending**: Three thumbprints, together. The door opens.

What's inside is remarkable. A fully stocked underground shelter - not a bunker, a home. Solar power, water reclamation, stored food for years, weapons, medical supplies. Raymond spent his fortune building this and told no one. He couldn't give his children a normal childhood. He gave them this instead.

There's a letter in the main room. Raymond's handwriting, shaky at the end:

*"I was a terrible father. I know it. I didn't know how to love small things, including children. I could only love big things - projects, plans, preparations. So I built you this. It's not enough. It's everything I had.*

*You'll hate each other on the drive here. You've hated each other for years, and that's my fault too. But I made it so you have to arrive together. I made it so you have to survive together. Because the only thing I know about love is that it's learned through necessity. I'm giving you the necessity.*

*The world outside is ending. The world in here can last, but only if you can stand to be family again. I can't make you try. I can only give you a place worth trying for.*

*Your father, who loved you badly but completely,*
*Raymond"*

They stay. Not because they've forgiven each other - forgiveness takes longer. Because they have nowhere else to go, and the alternative is dying alone.

Over months, the shelter becomes a base. They take in others - carefully, slowly. Diane handles organization. Marcus handles construction and defense. James, to everyone's surprise, becomes the diplomat - the youngest child who learned to read rooms becomes the one who decides who can be trusted.

They're still not a family in the warm sense. But they're a unit. They survive each other, and eventually, that becomes its own kind of bond.

The compound becomes known to travelers as "Price Station" - a waypoint in the desert, hostile to raiders, cautiously generous to refugees. Raymond's paranoid fortune becomes infrastructure for dozens of people he never met.

His children never quite forgive him. They don't need to. They're too busy running the thing he built.

### Themes Emerged
- Family trauma as survival obstacle
- Cooperation vs. individual interest
- Legacy and inheritance (literal and metaphorical)
- The dead still shaping the living
- Forced interdependence
- Small-scale community building

# Common Mechanics Emerging

After these five stories, patterns appear:

## Core Decision Types
1. **Resource trade-offs** (supplies vs. people vs. time vs. risk)
2. **Trust assessments** (strangers, groups, information)
3. **Route choices** (fast/dangerous vs. slow/safe vs. unknown)
4. **Moral dilemmas** (helping others vs. self-preservation)
5. **Internal conflicts** (group cohesion, leadership, roles)

## Essential Systems Needed
- **Travel/Fuel economy** - Every story revolves around movement
- **Vehicle condition tracking** - Breakdowns create tension
- **Character relationships** - Trust, conflict, bonds
- **Information gathering** - Knowing what's ahead matters
- **Resource management** - Food, water, medicine, fuel, ammo
- **Reputation/History** - Past actions affect future encounters
- **Skills and specialization** - Mechanics, doctors, scouts, diplomats

## What Makes Stories Compelling
- Specific characters with histories
- Impossible choices without clean answers
- Small victories inside larger struggles
- The journey changing the travelers
- Community emerging from strangers
- Hope that costs something

## Different Emotional Registers
- Story 1: Heavy, focused on care and loss
- Story 2: Action-oriented, focused on justice
- Story 3: Epic journey, focused on perseverance
- Story 4: Intellectual, focused on connection
- Story 5: Intimate, focused on family/forgiveness

A good game should hit all these notes at different times.
